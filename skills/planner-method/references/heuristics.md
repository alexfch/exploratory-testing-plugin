# Heuristics

## 1. Risk-Based Heuristics

| Heuristic | Definition | Intent | When to pick | Inputs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
|General risk framing|A risk is a potential problem. Frame each one in two steps. (1) Write it as a story: a threat (an input, condition or event, deliberate or accidental) triggers a vulnerability (a weakness in the product), causing a problem that harms a victim (a person, group or system that suffers inconvenience, damage or loss). For example: a timeout during checkout makes the app resend the payment (threat), the payment service doesn’t detect duplicates (vulnerability), and the customer (victim) is charged twice (problem). (2) Rate each risk’s impact and likelihood as high, medium or low, each with a one-line reason, then rank them into a risk list.|Turn vague worry into specific test ideas (make the threat happen, then look for the problem), and put testing effort where it matters most.|Before testing: when writing or choosing charters (each charter names the risks it targets) and when setting priorities. During testing: whenever you notice a new risk, write it as a story and add it to the risk list. After testing: when explaining why you tested one area and not another.|[Product areas or features in scope], [Who could be harmed (victims): users, customers, operators, the business, other systems], [What could trigger problems (threats): usage and misuse, unusual data, environments, load, failing or slow dependencies], [Likely weak spots (vulnerabilities): past failures and incidents, recent changes, complex or fragile parts], [Business impact of failure (if known)]|
|RCRCRC|A mnemonic for deciding where to focus regression testing (testing whether anything that used to work has broken or degraded). It points to areas, not to old tests to re-run. Recent: what changed lately, from new features to small changes like a logging update. Core: a quick pass over the happy paths of the few features that must work. Risky: areas where a failure is likely or costly, such as complex logic or features that depend on other services. Configuration-sensitive: behavior that depends on settings, such as feature flags, environment variables, account or tenant settings, locale or permissions. Repaired: the area around each recent fix, not just its original repro steps, since fixes often introduce new bugs in the same feature. Chronic: areas that keep breaking.|Choose a regression scope you can defend, tracing each area to the letters that put it in scope. Then test each area for anything that broke or degraded, varying your tests instead of only repeating old ones.|Before a release, or after a significant change (a merge, hotfix, dependency upgrade or configuration change), when deciding where regression testing should go.|[Recent: change log, commit list or release notes], [Core: the few features that must work], [Risky: the risk list from General risk framing], [Configuration-sensitive: feature flags, environment variables, account or tenant settings, locales, permissions, plus browsers or devices where behavior differs], [Repaired: recently fixed bugs and what each fix changed], [Chronic: defect history by area (if any)]|

## 2. Exploratory Testing Tactics

These are concrete *in-session* tactics, not coverage frameworks — use them to vary technique during hands-on exploration.

| Heuristic | Definition | Intent | When to pick | Inputs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
|Landmark Tour|Choose the key features (“landmarks”) and visit them in different sequences, as a tourist hops between sights.|Learn how the main features connect and find problems in transitions and ordering.|Early in a test cycle, when the product is unfamiliar but you have have landmarks provided.|[List of key features or landmarks], [feature relationships or navigation map], [entry points]|
|Money Tour|Exercise the features the product is sold on (what demos, marketing and sales promise), first as demonstrated, then like a skeptical customer asking “what if I…?” Not a tour of payment or pricing flows.|Find failures in the advertised capabilities, especially just off the demo path, and gaps between what’s promised and what the product does.|A demo, launch or major release is coming, or headline features or marketing claims have changed.|Marketing pages and feature lists], [Sales demo scripts or recordings], [Release announcements], [Sales or customer priorities (if any)]|
|FedEx Tour|Follow a piece of data through the system from entry to exit, checking what happens at each stop.|Find data loss, corruption, transformation, and handoff problems.|Integrations, pipelines, reports, imports/exports, or multi-step workflows.|[Data entities to trace], [system or component map], [sample data sets], [places data is stored or displayed]|
|Guidebook Tour|Follow the official documentation literally, step by step.|Find mismatches between documented and actual behavior, plus onboarding and documentation bugs.|Docs exist and matter, new users rely on tutorials, or you suspect docs are stale.|[User manual, help pages, tutorials, release notes], [version of the docs], [stated limits or claims]|
|Intellectual Tour|Ask the software the hardest questions you can think of: what makes the software work hardest, and which inputs push it to its limits.|Find weaknesses in complex logic, rules, and edge cases.|Basic flows are understood and you want depth over breadth.|[Business rules or logic], [boundary and extreme values], [complex scenarios or combinations], [known tricky areas]|
|Rumor Tour|Collect what people say or suspect about the product (“exports sometimes drop rows”, “nobody trusts the sync”) and test each claim as a hypothesis.|Settle each rumor: reproduce it, show it doesn’t hold (and what you tried), or find the real problem behind it.|Support, sales or teammates report problems nobody has reproduced, an area has a bad reputation, or a release is near and “known” issues haven’t been checked.|[Support tickets and customer complaints], [Notes from sales, support, team chat or interviews], [Reviews or forum posts (if any)], [Related bug reports or incidents, to pin a rumor down (if any)]|
|Steeplechase Heuristic|When testing a boundary, treat the data as running an obstacle course: it has to reach the boundary you’re targeting, then clear every later obstacle as other parts of the system process it.|Find what a single boundary check misses. That means extreme values that fail at an earlier obstacle before the limit is reached (e.g., a million-character paste into a 1,000-character field), and values that pass the limit but get mangled downstream.|Inside any session that probes a limit (length, size, range, count), especially when the data flows on to storage, other services, reports or exports.|[Known or suspected limits, and where each is enforced], [The data’s downstream path: storage, services, reports, exports], [Values at, just past and far beyond each limit], [Ways to observe downstream: logs, stored records, outputs]|
|Galumphing|Carry out a test in a deliberately over-elaborate way, adding cheap, supposedly harmless actions that shouldn’t change the outcome (clicking empty space on the way to a button, extra whitespace, redundant steps).|Expose hidden sensitivity to actions that supposedly don’t matter, adding lots of variation at almost no cost.|Inside any session, while executing tests, especially once a flow passes in its plain form or you’ve run the same path more than once without varying it.|[A flow or action that works in its plain form], [Harmless-seeming extras: idle clicks, extra whitespace, redundant steps, unused optional parameters], [The interfaces you can vary: UI, API, CLI]|
|Survey / Reconnaissance Testing|A broad, shallow first pass to learn what the product is and does (features, data, users, risky areas) before deciding where to test deeply.|Produce a written map of the product, a list of risks and questions, and charter ideas for deeper sessions. Learning comes first: note problems you see, but don’t stop to chase them.|Start of a test cycle, a product or area that’s new to you, after a major change, or when you don’t yet know which features are the key ones.|[Build or environment access], [User stories, specs, docs or help (if any)], [Existing feature list or navigation map (if any)]|
|Focusing / Defocusing|Deliberately alternate between two ways of testing. Focusing narrows to one thing (a field, a rule, a claim, a failure) and controls the conditions around it: start from a known, clean state, use simple and repeatable actions, change one factor at a time, and make specific predictions and precise records. Defocusing brings more factors in: start from different, not necessarily clean, states; change several factors at once; use complex, challenging actions; generate tests from different models; and watch for problems other than the ones you’re looking for. It still calls for careful observation, just across a wider field. Defocus within the charter first, and note any detour beyond it.|Each mode makes up for the other’s weakness. Focusing turns an observation into a precise, reproducible finding and covers one part meticulously, preventing vague results nobody can reproduce. Defocusing finds unexpected and elusive problems (ones that appear only under messy, combined or sustained conditions) and breaks repetitive patterns, preventing tunnel vision.|Focus when something looks wrong and you need to reproduce, isolate or describe it precisely, when you’re testing a specific hypothesis, or when the charter calls for meticulous coverage of one part. Defocus when your last several tests followed the same pattern and turned up nothing new, when you run out of test ideas, or when a problem won’t reproduce under controlled conditions (add complexity, such as a dirty state, several changes at once or load, until it recurs, then focus to isolate it). Stop focusing when isolation stops narrowing things down or is using up the time-box: record what you know and what you’ve ruled out, then return to the charter. When chartering, include some broad charters that aren’t aimed at a specific risk alongside risk-focused ones.|[Current charter and risks], [Observations and anomalies so far, with the exact steps and conditions behind each], [For focusing: the thing under scrutiny, a known starting state and a way to return to it, and the factors you can control (data, settings, sequence, timing, environment)], [For defocusing: factors you haven’t varied yet (state, data, sequence, timing, load, configuration, user role), other models to generate tests from (data flows, state transitions, user scenarios), and other kinds of problems to watch for], [Time-box and time used so far]|
